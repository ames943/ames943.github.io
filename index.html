I'm a student at Santa Clara High School. I'm interested in computer science and use it in my research in biology and economics.

My main project is in computational biology. Studies of gut bacteria and cancer immunotherapy often pool public datasets from several studies to get more patients. I ran four of these datasets through one pipeline and found that which study a patient came from explained far more of the variation than whether the patient responded to treatment. A short version of the paper was accepted to an IEEE BIBM 2026 workshop, and I released the tool I built for it, batch_detector, as open-source Python.

I also work on economic history with Thomas J. Sargent (NYU) and George J. Hall (Brandeis), using 1840s bond prices to study what happened to cities when their states defaulted.

Cohort Effects, Not Treatment Response, Dominate Public Immunotherapy Cohorts: Evidence from Microbiome and Tumor Genomic Data

Pooling four public gut microbiome cohorts (283 patients on immune checkpoint drugs), the study a sample came from explained 6 to 9.5 percentage points of variation beyond chance. Treatment response explained no more than chance. Classifiers stayed at chance and six batch-correction methods recovered nothing. In three tumor genomic cohorts, the study effect was about eighteen times the response effect.

Does Default Stay Local? State Default and City Credit in the 1840s

When Pennsylvania stopped paying its debts in 1842, its borrowing costs rose. Philadelphia's and Pittsburgh's did not. Using weekly bond prices from the 1830s to the 1850s, I find that investors priced each city on its own finances rather than its state's.

Software

batch_detector

A single Python file to run before pooling datasets. You give it a table of samples, the study each one came from, and the outcome you care about. It reports how much variation each of those explains, corrected for chance, and returns GO, CAUTION or NO-GO for pooling.
